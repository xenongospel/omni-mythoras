## 1. Master of Peace synthesis

Master of Peace (MoP) is scoped narrowly here: VFX/tiling system, general aesthetics, and battle systems. Explicitly **not** its UI layout — you've said you're not a fan of it and don't want it treated as a layout reference, so nothing below should be read as "adopt MoP's screen composition."

### 1.1 VFX and tiling system

**What it is:** MoP's combat plays out on a discrete grid/tile battlefield where ability effects — status auras, zone hazards, area templates, movement telegraphs — are rendered as tile-anchored VFX rather than free-floating particle spam. Skills read as *occupying space on the board*, not just animating over a character model. The tiling system means the game can communicate "this area is now dangerous" or "this tile is buffed" as a first-class visual object that persists and is legible at a glance, independent of whether a unit is currently standing on it.

**What makes this good design (not just what it is):**
- **Legibility under load.** A grid-based VFX language means the player never has to parse "is that particle effect decorative or does it represent a mechanical zone?" — tile-anchored effects are unambiguously mechanical. This is the single hardest problem in turn-based tactics UI: separating juice from information.
- **Persistence as a design surface.** Because effects live on tiles (not on transient particle systems tied to a cast animation), they can outlast the turn that created them — a burning tile, a warded tile, a difficult-terrain tile — which turns VFX into gameplay state the player can *plan around*, not just react to.
- **Decoupling of "where" from "who."** A tile-anchored hazard is readable even if the unit that created it dies, moves, or is off-screen. This is a robustness property: the board's state stays comprehensible independent of unit tracking.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** Mythoras is not confirmed to use a discrete tile grid (it's turn-based squad combat with a Turn Program, not stated to be positional-grid tactics) — so the literal tiling system may not map 1:1. But the underlying principle transfers directly: **any effect that has ongoing mechanical consequence (a debuff zone, a persistent Protocol effect, a "next enemy action" telegraph) should get a distinct, spatially-anchored visual treatment that reads as different in kind from a cast animation.** Concretely: if Mythoras ever has area effects, buffs-on-position, or persistent battlefield states, they should render as anchored plane/decal geometry (à la MoP's tile highlights) rather than particle bursts, so "this is now true about the world" is visually distinguishable from "this just happened once." Given the "Protocol" framing (compiled/structural power, not generic elemental magic), this pairs unusually well — a Protocol effect that's "installed" on a tile or unit slot could visually read as a persistent geometric overlay (a restrained glyph/outline, not a screen-filling particle wash), which is both on-brand and cheap to prototype as a colored plane with an outline shader.

### 1.2 General aesthetics

**What it is:** MoP's visual identity leans into restrained, atmospheric fantasy — mood and readability over maximalist detail density. Its palette and lighting favor legible silhouettes and a coherent tonal register (not the loud, high-saturation "gacha mobile" look common in the genre), which is part of why the owner flags it as an aesthetic reference at all despite disliking its UI.

**What makes this good design:** Restraint is a force multiplier for a small team. A coherent, muted palette with a small number of accent colors reads as "art directed" even with simple geometry, because consistency does more perceptual work than fidelity. This is the same principle Thronefall's devs land on independently (see §5) — "be cheap so you can be generous," i.e., a simple, consistent visual grammar lets you make a lot of content without every asset needing bespoke high effort.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** Mythoras should establish a small, disciplined palette early — probably anchored by whatever color mapping eventually gets assigned to AUTH/INV/ANIM/ORD (currently undecided per project canon) — and enforce it as a constraint on every asset, not an afterthought applied at polish time. A limited palette is also directly compatible with the "prototype rough and fast" philosophy: flat-shaded low-poly geometry with 4–6 disciplined hues reads as intentional far sooner than realistic materials would, and it's cheap to iterate on before any real art pipeline exists.

### 1.3 Battle systems

**What it is:** MoP's combat is turn-based/tactical with clear action economy, positioning, and a legible telegraph of enemy intent — the player is making informed tactical decisions each turn, not reacting blind.

**What makes this good design:** The core virtue here — shared with Slay the Spire's "intent" system (§5) and Darkest Dungeon's positional combat — is that **turn-based combat lives or dies on whether the player can reason about consequences before committing.** A battle system that hides too much (enemy intent, effect duration, damage ranges) collapses into guesswork; one that shows too much collapses into a spreadsheet. MoP's battle systems are cited specifically as a reference for calibrating that line well.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** This validates a design commitment Mythoras's Turn Program already implies structurally: Planning Phase should show enough — target, ability, and *some* signal of resolution order/enemy intent — that queuing 3 Field creatures' actions feels like a real tactical decision, not a blind commit. The exact information density (exact damage numbers vs. banded/symbolic, as Slay the Spire iterated through in real time — see §5.1) is an open question that should be **prototyped, not decided on paper**: it's cheap to build a debug-text version of "next enemy action" before any art exists, and MoP's calibration is useful evidence that "some enemy intent visibility" is table stakes for the genre, not a nice-to-have.

## 2. Darkest Dungeon synthesis

The compelling piece here is explicitly **split focus as a UI principle** — a deliberate division of the player's attention between a scene and a distinct information zone — not Darkest Dungeon's specific 60/40 horizontal ratio, nor its ink/parchment visual treatment. Other constructions (vertical splits, RuneScape-style dockable floating panels, or something DD itself never tried) are all still on the table.

### 2.1 Split focus as a principle

**What it is:** Darkest Dungeon never lets the 3D-ish diorama of the dungeon corridor be the *only* thing on screen. There's always a second zone — the party roster with portraits, HP, stress, and buffs — visually distinct from the "world" the camera is looking at. The player's eye is deliberately trained to move between "what is happening" (the scene) and "what does my squad's state mean right now" (the info zone), and the game never asks you to open a menu to get that second read.

**What makes this good design (not just what it is):** This solves a problem that is *structural* to squad-based RPGs: the state that matters most (each unit's HP, buffs, position, resources) cannot be represented believably as part of the diegetic scene without either (a) covering the scene in floating health bars and icon soup, or (b) hiding it behind a menu the player has to interrupt play to check. Split focus is the third option — a standing, non-diegetic zone that's always present but visually separated, so the "world" stays clean and the "state" stays legible, permanently, without competing for the same pixels or forcing a context switch. The *specific* ratio and orientation DD chose is a decorating decision; the *decision to divide attention this way at all* is the transferable insight.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** Mythoras's squad-of-5 (3 Field + 2 Bench) with a Turn Program planning phase is exactly the shape of problem split focus solves. The layout question ("horizontal like DD, vertical, floating dockable panels RuneScape-style, something else entirely") is a real open design question and should be treated as one — genuinely worth prototyping two or three layout skeletons in the existing Three.js scaffold with placeholder boxes before committing, since layout is cheap to mock and expensive to get wrong later once real UI code depends on it. What's *not* open: some non-diegetic, always-visible zone showing squad state should exist, distinct from the battle scene itself, so a player queuing actions for 3 Field creatures during the Planning Phase never has to hunt for HP/status information inside the 3D scene.

### 2.2 The roster band that never disappears

**What it is:** Already established in prior Mythoras research — DD's roster strip persists across camp, travel, and combat as one continuous, unbroken UI element. It's not "the combat HUD" and "the camp menu" as two different things that happen to show similar data; it's the *same* element, present everywhere.

**What makes this good design:** Continuity of a UI element across game modes does real cognitive work — the player never has to relearn where to look for "is my squad okay" depending on what screen they're on. It also reinforces that the squad *is* the throughline of the game (mechanically and emotionally), which matters more in a roster-management RPG than in almost any other genre, because attachment to specific units is the retention hook.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** Carry this forward as-is. Whatever the split-focus info zone ends up looking like, it should be the *same* component whether the player is in camp, in a menu, or mid-combat — not a combat-only overlay that vanishes elsewhere. This is cheap to enforce from day one architecturally (one shared UI component/state store for squad status) and expensive to retrofit later, so it belongs in the prototype's structural decisions now, even before it has real visual polish.

### 2.3 Position-in-line as a real mechanical fact

**What it is:** In DD, where a character stands in the marching order isn't cosmetic — front rank vs. back rank changes what abilities are usable and what damage a unit is exposed to. Position is a first-class mechanical variable, not a visual arrangement.

**What makes this good design:** It turns squad composition into a spatial puzzle in addition to a "what stats/skills" puzzle, and it makes the *diorama itself* — not just menus — a place where strategy happens. This is part of why DD's "look" (a shallow horizontal stage) isn't just an aesthetic choice — it's load-bearing for the position mechanic.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** Mythoras already has the raw material for this: 3 Field + 2 Bench is a positional distinction (who's exposed to enemy action vs. who's in reserve). The open design question is whether Field itself has *internal* position (front/back/left/right within the 3) that matters mechanically, or whether Field vs. Bench is the only positional axis that matters. This is a cheap thing to prototype with the current placeholder cylinder markers — before any art exists, you can test "does moving markers around within Field change anything the player cares about" as a pure logic experiment. Given Skills are equippable objects (not a fixed action bar), there's a natural hook: certain skills could require or reward specific Field positions, the same way DD's classes are role-locked to ranks.

### 2.4 Painterly mood carried by art, not UI chrome volume

**What it is:** DD's oppressive, grim mood comes from illustration, lighting, and audio — not from the UI having lots of ink-splatter borders and gothic frame ornamentation. If you stripped the ink/parchment chrome down to plain rectangles, the game would still feel grim because the *content* (art, writing, sound) carries the tone.

**What makes this good design:** This is a durability property — chrome-heavy UI ages badly, is expensive to reskin, and doesn't survive a rebrand or an art pass; mood carried by content survives UI changes because it isn't coupled to them. It's also directly compatible with the "prototype rough and fast" philosophy already established for Mythoras: a placeholder-flat UI doesn't undermine the mood as long as the *scene* (creature models, lighting, VFX) is doing the tonal work.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** Do not try to manufacture Mythoras's tone through UI ornamentation (borders, frame flourishes, decorative iconography) — that's exactly the kind of polish investment the owner wants to defer. Put tonal weight on creature design, environment art, and combat VFX instead, and let the UI stay plain and functional for as long as possible. This is good news for the prototype stage specifically: it means the current bare Three.js scaffold isn't "failing" to convey mood by being undecorated — it's simply not yet at the stage where mood-bearing elements (creatures, VFX) exist at all, which is a sequencing fact, not a tone problem.

### 2.5 Full-screen dedicated moments instead of permanent side panels

**What it is:** DD dedicates full-screen treatment to certain moments — the stagecoach/roster screen, the provisioning screen, the sanitarium — rather than cramming every system into a permanent sidebar that's always partially visible.

**What makes this good design:** Not every system deserves permanent screen real estate. Full-screen dedicated moments let a system (e.g., gearing, provisioning) get focused, uncluttered treatment exactly when the player is engaging with it, without that system's UI competing for space the rest of the time. This is a scope-control tool as much as a UX one — it means a system's UI can be built and iterated on in isolation.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** Given that Skills are equippable objects assigned to a creature "like gear," a Mythoras loadout/gearing screen is a strong candidate for full-screen dedicated treatment (echoing PoE's inventory-and-passive-tree screens being their own dedicated space, not a corner widget — see §3) rather than trying to squeeze skill-assignment into the split-focus combat zone. This also keeps the combat split-focus zone lean, since it doesn't need to also handle deep inventory/build management.

## 3. Path of Exile synthesis

Path of Exile (PoE) is the one reference explicitly scoped for **full progression coverage**, not narrowed to a favorite piece. Each subsystem below: what it is, what makes it good design, and a Mythoras-native equivalent grounded in the underlying principle — particularly with SSF (solo self-found) replay depth as the throughline, since that's the play mode most relevant to a squad-RPG's single-player retention problem.

### 3.1 The gearing system

**What it is:** Gear in PoE has a base type (which sets implicit properties and a socket/quality ceiling) plus a large, mostly-open pool of prefix/suffix affixes rolled within ranges, plus rarity tiers (normal/magic/rare/unique) that gate how many affixes a given item can carry. A "good" item isn't just "high level" — it's a specific combination of base + affixes + rolls that supports a specific build.

**What makes this good design:** Gear becomes a *search space*, not a checklist. Because affixes are drawn from a large pool with meaningful ranges, no two rare items are quite the same, which makes gear itself a source of build-defining decisions rather than a numbers-go-up treadmill. It also means gear has resale/trade value that reflects genuine rarity of a *specific* useful combination, not just item level — which matters enormously in SSF, where the player can't trade and instead has to make peace with (or intentionally chase) their own drop luck.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** Skills being equippable objects assigned to a creature "like gear" already borrows PoE's base-item framing at the skill level rather than a separate gear slot — which is a distinctive design choice worth leaning into further rather than bolting a second, separate PoE-style gear system on top. The transferable principle: **make the equippable-skill pool wide and combinatorially interesting enough that assigning skills to creatures is itself the "gearing" decision space**, with variance (skill quality tiers, rolled sub-stats on a skill instance, rarity of specific skill+stat combinations) doing the job PoE's affix system does. This is cheap to prototype as pure data/math — before any art exists, you can validate whether "which skills go on which of my 5 creatures" produces PoE-style decision density using nothing but a spreadsheet-style debug UI.

### 3.2 Crafting / "grafting" steps

**What it is:** PoE's crafting isn't a single action — it's a sequence of reversible-ish and irreversible steps (e.g., applying a currency, then locking a result, then applying another) where the player is making a plan across multiple resource-consuming steps toward a target outcome, with real risk of failure or of "crafting away" a good roll.

**What makes this good design:** Multi-step crafting turns itemization from a passive drop-and-equip loop into an active minigame with its own skill ceiling — experienced players extract more value from the same raw materials than new players, which is a durable form of expressible mastery that doesn't require twitch skill. It also creates a market/economy in trade-enabled contexts and a satisfying "I built this" feeling in SSF specifically, since every crafted item is provably the product of the player's own choices and luck.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** If skills are equippable objects with rollable sub-properties (per §3.1), a Mythoras-native "grafting" step could let the player spend resources to modify an existing skill instance in controlled, sequenced ways — reroll a sub-stat, lock a favored roll before rerolling the rest, upgrade a skill's tier — rather than a single "salvage and reroll" black box. The key design property to preserve is **sequenced, partially-reversible decision-making with real risk**, not any specific currency-orb mechanic. This is a system that can be prototyped entirely in isolation from the 3D game — a numbers-and-buttons harness — before it needs any visual identity at all, which fits the "cheap to test whether the fundamental feels right" filter well.

### 3.3 The orb system / currency-as-crafting-material

**What it is:** PoE's defining twist is that its "currency" (gold-equivalent) *is* its crafting material — orbs are simultaneously the economy's medium of exchange and the tool used to modify gear. There's no separate "gold" that's disconnected from itemization.

**What makes this good design:** Collapsing currency and crafting material into one resource means every drop is legible in two ways at once — its trade value and its direct utility — which keeps the loot feed interesting even for items and materials the player has no board use for right now (they're still economically meaningful). It also means the economy is emergent from crafting scarcity rather than an arbitrarily tuned gold-sink, which tends to self-balance better over a game's lifetime.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** This is a genuinely risky pattern to copy literally (PoE's economy took years of live tuning and an active trade community to work), and the brief warns against porting systems beat-for-beat — so the right takeaway is narrower: **consider whether Mythoras's crafting/grafting resource (§3.2) should double as something the player wants regardless of immediate use**, rather than inventing a wholly separate gold currency that's disconnected from the skill-grafting economy. Whether that's literally tradeable in an SSF-first single-player context is a separate, much bigger question (multiplayer/economy is out of scope for a prototype) — but the *design shape* (fewer disconnected resource types, each with dual utility) is worth keeping in mind when the resource list starts growing.

### 3.4 The passive tree

**What it is:** A large, mostly-shared skill tree with thousands of small nodes offering incremental stat bonuses and a smaller number of larger "keystone" nodes that meaningfully alter how a build plays, navigated by spending points earned from leveling and quests. Different classes start at different points on the same tree.

**What makes this good design:** The tree does two jobs at once: it's a *visualization* of a build's identity (you can look at someone's tree shape and read what kind of character they're playing) and it's a *long-horizon goal structure* (there's always a next node worth pathing to). Its size is itself a feature — the tree being mostly the same object for every class means player knowledge (pathing efficiency, keystone synergies) transfers across characters, which rewards system mastery independent of any one build.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** A per-creature or per-squad passive-progression structure is a plausible long-horizon retention system for Mythoras, but the tree's *scale* is the part most likely to be a trap for a small team — PoE's tree is a decade-plus live-service asset. A Mythoras-native version should almost certainly be **much smaller and squad/roster-scoped rather than per-creature-massive**, preserving the two functional properties (build gets a visible "shape," there's always a next meaningful choice) without committing to thousands of nodes. This is explicitly *not* a first-prototype priority (see §6) — it's a mid-game-loop structure that only matters once the moment-to-moment combat is already validated.

### 3.5 The campaign → endgame transition

**What it is:** PoE's campaign (a linear story progression through acts) is explicitly a tutorial and character-building funnel, not the game's replay loop. The "real game" is the endgame — the Atlas — which the campaign exists to deliver the player into.

**What makes this good design:** Separating "the part that teaches you the game and builds one character" from "the part you replay indefinitely" lets each be optimized for its own job. The campaign can afford to be linear, curated, and skippable-feeling on repeat characters (PoE has iterated hard on shortening it) precisely because it's not competing with the endgame for the player's long-term attention. This is a scope-management insight as much as a player-facing one: a team doesn't have to make its onboarding content *and* its infinite replay content serve the same design goals simultaneously.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** Plan for two explicitly different design modes from early on, even if only one gets built first: an onboarding/story mode that teaches squad-building and Turn Program mechanics once, and a separate, replayable "endgame" loop (see §3.6) that the story mode feeds into. Given the prototype stage, the honest sequencing is: **validate the endgame replay loop's core feel first**, since that's the system the game lives or dies on long-term, and treat the campaign/onboarding wrapper as a later, lower-risk problem (it's mostly content and pacing work, not core-systems risk).

### 3.6 Endgame fixtures and SSF replay depth

**What it is:** PoE's endgame stacks several interlocking systems on top of the base "kill things, get loot" loop — the Atlas (a map of run nodes with their own modifiers), league mechanics (rotating seasonal systems layered onto maps), and various optional deep-dive systems (delving, heist-style side activities, etc.) — all of which are relevant to SSF specifically because they generate *player-controlled* variance and goals without requiring trade: a solo player can always choose to make the next map harder/weirder/more rewarding, and craft toward a goal using only their own drops.

**What makes this good design (for SSF particularly):** The core insight is that replay depth in a solo context comes from **player-adjustable risk/reward knobs the player controls directly**, not from external content drip alone. A player who's "done" the story can still meaningfully choose to dial difficulty and modifiers up for better rewards, chase a specific crafting or itemization goal using only self-found materials, and feel like progress is self-directed rather than gated by content cadence.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** This is probably the single most important PoE lesson for Mythoras's long-term shape, precisely because Mythoras (per the fixed facts) has no stated multiplayer/trade plans — it's implicitly an SSF-shaped game already. A Mythoras endgame loop should give the player direct knobs to escalate difficulty/reward on repeatable content (not just a fixed difficulty curve that ends), and let self-found skill/creature acquisition be enough to chase specific build goals without needing external trade. This is explicitly a later-stage system, not a prototype-now priority — but it's worth naming now so early architecture (how skills/creatures are generated and stored) doesn't foreclose it later. Concretely: keep skill/creature generation data-driven and parameterized (rarity tiers, roll ranges) from day one, because retrofitting "generate variance" onto a hand-authored system later is much more expensive than building on top of a parameterized one from the start.

## 4. Pokémon synthesis

Scoped narrowly, per the brief: **narrative/brand premise only**, not mechanics or art style.

**What it is:** A trainer-and-squad structure inside an athletic-tournament journey — the player is a person traveling a world, building a team of creatures through relationships and effort, competing in a structured, ascending series of contests, in a world that (unlike Pokémon's own bright, kid-friendly art direction) should read as more grounded/real for Mythoras.

**What makes this a good narrative premise:** The trainer-and-squad frame does real emotional work independent of art style or difficulty: it gives the player a reason to *care about specific individual creatures* (not just "my roster's average power level"), it gives structural pacing for free (tournament arcs create natural difficulty ramps and narrative stakes without inventing a new plot device), and "athletic journey" framing makes escalating challenge feel earned and diegetic rather than arbitrary ("the next gym is harder" reads as natural, the way "the next dungeon has bigger numbers" doesn't always).

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** Use the trainer-and-squad, tournament-journey *shape* to inform tone and stakes — the player should feel like they're building relationships with a specific 3+2 squad over a journey with real milestones, in a world grounded enough that losing a creature or a fight has weight (closer to Darkest Dungeon's stakes than Pokémon's own consequence-light tone). This is a framing device more than a system: it should inform how the campaign/story mode (§3.5) is paced and staged, and how creature acquisition/loss is written, rather than dictating any mechanic. It pairs naturally with the DD principle of a persistent roster band (§2.2) — if the roster is always visible and the narrative treats squad members as individuals with a journey, those two choices reinforce each other for free.

## 5. Dev-philosophy synthesis

Sources: Slay the Spire dev philosophy retrospective, the Slay the Spire 2 reverse-engineered code review, five Thronefall devlogs (0–4), and the Godot project-structure video. All were watched/read in full (transcripts + sampled frame contact sheets), not summarized from titles.

### 5.1 Slay the Spire: play-test data over intuition, and iterate the *interface to information* relentlessly

The retrospective's most concrete throughline is that Mega Crit treated their two-person QA background as a competitive advantage: they built an internal metrics server (via Anthony's wife, who had big-data experience) that logged every play-tester decision from day one, and used it to catch problems intuition missed — e.g., data showed players would find one strong card, upgrade it indefinitely, and stop exploring alternatives, which led to the one-upgrade-per-card rule, which then felt too restrictive, which led to Anthony and Casey spending "eight straight hours" hand-designing a unique upgrade for every single card. That's a concrete illustration of iterate-fast-on-real-feedback rather than solve-it-on-paper.

The second throughline, and the one most directly relevant to Mythoras's Turn Program, is the multi-pass iteration on **how much combat information to show**. They went through at minimum four distinct designs for enemy-intent visibility: (1) no visibility at all — copying a JRPG model, which failed because losing in a roguelike is catastrophic in a way JRPG battles aren't, so guessing felt bad, not exciting; (2) a "next turn" system with full text description of enemy plans, which worked informationally but required clicking each enemy individually and felt overwhelming with multiple enemies; (3) a symbolic/banded system (weapon icons implying damage *ranges*, not exact numbers) which tested well with the developers themselves (Casey specifically liked staying in a fast "flow state" without tracking numbers) but tested *badly* with outside players, who over-analyzed trying to decode the symbols; (4) the final version — exact damage numbers directly displayed — which testers immediately preferred because it removed guesswork for newcomers without penalizing veterans. The lesson is not "show exact numbers" as a rule; it's that **the developers' own preference (flow-state, minimal numbers) was wrong for their actual audience, and they only found that out by testing outside their own two-person bubble.**

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** Build the cheapest possible version of "how much do I show about enemy actions during the Planning Phase" — plain debug text is enough — and get it in front of people who are not you before committing to a final visual language for intent/telegraph. This is *the* cheap-to-prototype, reveals-core-feel item from this whole reference set, because Slay the Spire's own history proves the "right" answer isn't derivable from taste alone, even for a team as design-literate as Mega Crit.

### 5.2 Slay the Spire 2's code review: what a decade of real production code protects against

The reverse-engineered architecture talk is less about philosophy and more a grounded look at what a mature, live, financially successful codebase actually spends effort on, which is informative by contrast with a fresh prototype:

- **Determinism as an explicit design constraint once multiplayer exists**: they use `decimal` instead of `float`/`int` for all in-game math specifically to avoid cross-hardware floating-point desync, and they have separate "stable shuffle" (deterministic, sorts first) vs. "unstable shuffle" functions depending on whether determinism across clients matters for a given operation.
- **A dumb-on-purpose automated QA bot** ("Auto Slayer") that plays thousands of seeded runs by picking uniformly random valid choices rather than "smart" choices — because random play surfaces edge-case bugs that optimal play never reaches. This is a cheap, high-leverage QA tool that doesn't require an AI or a human.
- **A first-run scripted tutorial hidden inside otherwise-random systems** — new players' first few map choices, and their first treasure chest's contents, are quietly hard-coded rather than random, specifically to guarantee a curated first impression, invisibly.
- **A found, real bug** (multi-card-selection via keyboard arrow-key input bypassing a `maxSelect` check that mouse input respected) that existed because validation logic was duplicated across three near-identical screen classes instead of centralized — a concrete, unglamorous illustration of exactly the kind of technical debt the Godot structure video (§5.5) is trying to prevent architecturally.

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** None of this is relevant to Mythoras *yet* (no multiplayer, no live economy, no need for `decimal` math) — but two ideas are worth banking for later at low cost now: (1) a dumb-random automated playtester is a cheap, high-value tool to build once there's a real Turn Program loop to fuzz, well before any human playtesting infrastructure exists; (2) if/when Mythoras has any UI screen that repeats similar interaction logic in more than one place (e.g., multiple "assign skill to creature" screens), centralize the validation now rather than copy-pasting it, because that exact anti-pattern is what caused SotS2's real production bug.

### 5.3 Thronefall devlogs 0–2: the failed prototype is the point, not a detour

The devlog arc is unusually candid about failure, and it's the strongest real-world evidence available for the brief's stated thesis (Thronefall's dev failed to prototype enough on a previous game, and now over-prototypes deliberately):

- **Devlog 0** documents ~6 weeks of pure idea generation and rapid, disposable prototyping *before* picking a direction — analog card prototypes tested with real people for a deck-building kingdom-management idea, a procedural terrain generator built as a pure technical feasibility test (not tied to any specific game), a physical (non-digital) card prototype used specifically to test whether "dragging characters around" was fun *before* any code existed. Nine "killer ideas" were narrowed to one using explicit criteria (does it play to the team's actual strengths, e.g. "no dedicated artist this time" killed an idea that needed one) rather than raw enthusiasm.
- **Devlog 1** is the pivotal failure: the team spent 30 days building the chosen idea (a card-based kingdom-management game) as if it were validated, then discovered the core gameplay "felt more like a math problem than an actual game." Their own stated diagnosis: *"we broke the rule — we did not fail fast. We should have done more prototyping, do a quick and dirty version of the game instead. Instead we jumped right into the main development."* They explicitly credit their co-founder relationship for catching the mistake before more time was sunk, and reframe the 30 days as valuable input rather than pure waste — the two things they kept were the *fantasy* (medieval kingdom management) and the discipline of "do one thing and do it well," while discarding the card-based system entirely in favor of a tower-defense-shaped kingdom management loop that became Thronefall.
- **Devlog 2** shows the resulting rapid-fire art and mechanics validation cycle once the direction was locked: a same-day art pass gets an outside friend's honest "that looks like a cool start" (meant as praise, received as "we thought this was finished — back to the drawing board"), followed by a full early-game/late-game gameplay-only prototype built *before* any real production commitment, explicitly to "know for sure if this works" — described as deliberately "quick and dirty," not "gonna look great."

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** The devlogs are direct, first-party confirmation of the exact philosophy already given as the frame for this research: rough, disposable, single-question prototypes *before* committing to a system, tested with people outside the founding team where possible, are cheaper than they feel and catch failures that intuition and internal enthusiasm both miss. The specific, transferable process detail worth copying is Thronefall's discipline of **stating the prototype's question before building it** ("we wanted to figure out if the simple interaction of dragging characters around was fun enough") — this is a cheap habit to adopt immediately for Mythoras's own prototyping (see §6), and it's the difference between a prototype that answers something and a demo that just exists.

### 5.4 Thronefall devlogs 3–4: "fix fun first," honest balancing, and killing features that don't survive contact with players

- **Devlog 3** contains the explicit line (said half-jokingly, then embraced): *"our main priority is really to get the gameplay right — game developers always say 'fix fun first.'"* It also documents real balancing philosophy: *"a common misconception people have about balancing is that it's about making everything equally powerful, whereas it's actually not about that at all — balancing in strategy games is way more about carving out a niche for everything and making everything situational."* Every unit/tool should be useful *some* of the time, not all-things-equal.
- **Devlog 4** is the most valuable evidence for "playtest with real outsiders, then actually change the design" — after external friend playtesting, several designed features were killed or reworked because they looked good on paper and felt bad in practice: wandering idle villagers (tried four variations, all "annoying and distracting," ultimately reverted — *"sometimes doing nothing is actually best"*); visible enemy spawn tents around the player's base (looked "cool" at first glance, but "destroys a lot of the coziness," reverted). Conversely, features playtesters explicitly asked for were added even late: a unit-command system (added on day 100, acknowledged as requiring rebalancing "almost everything," but judged worth it — *"the playtest was 100% the correct call. I'm so happy with it"*) and build-slot customization variety, because players complained the castle "felt too similar each time."

**Mythoras-native takeaway:** Two disciplines worth adopting explicitly for Mythoras: (1) treat "fix fun first" as literal sequencing — the Turn Program's moment-to-moment feel (queuing actions, seeing them resolve) should be validated with placeholder boxes before any art, skill depth, or progression system gets built on top of it, because none of those layers matter if the core turn loop isn't fun; (2) balance for *niche*, not *parity* — with only 4 attributes (AUTH/INV/ANIM/ORD) and a small Field/Bench squad, the temptation will be to make every skill/creature "fair" against every other; Thronefall's lesson is that the better goal is making sure every skill/creature has *some* situation where it's clearly the right call, which is both more interesting and more forgiving to balance solo.

### 5.5 The Godot project-structure video: the answer to "should we build our own pseudo-engine"

The video is a practical, code-first walkthrough (not a philosophy talk) of setting up ownership boundaries in a growing Godot project — but its underlying argument transfers directly to the "should Mythoras build its own pseudo-engine" question the owner raised, because it's really an argument about *when structure needs to exist*, independent of what engine or stack is underneath.

The core argument: a project doesn't fail because it lacks a full engine's worth of features — it fails because **ownership boundaries between pieces (who owns the player, who owns the level, who owns transitions, who coordinates between them) aren't decided before the pieces start talking to each other**, at which point "it becomes obvious why it's called spaghetti code." The video's concrete structure — a coordinator root (`main game`) that creates/owns high-level containers (world, HUD, pause, transitions, debug) but does not micromanage what's inside them; levels that own their own geometry/spawn points but *don't* own the player (they expose a spawn marker instead, so the level "doesn't need to know which player scene to instantiate"); a stable single entry point (`load_level()`) that every caller goes through regardless of what triggers it (main menu, a cave entrance, etc.) — is a general pattern, not a Godot-specific one. Its explicit thesis: *"one of the most important things you can do for your project is set up a solid foundation before it starts growing in every direction... I have learned the hard way what happens when you code first and plan later."*

**Answering the "build our own pseudo-engine" question directly, as asked:** No — not now, and probably not as a deliberate goal even later. Here's the reasoning:

1. **What Mythoras actually needs from "engine-like" structure is ownership boundaries and a few reusable systems (scene/level loading, an entity registry, a UI layer stack, a pause/transition-safe update loop) — not engine *features* (a physics engine, a full editor, asset pipelines, a scripting VM).** The Godot video's whole argument is that these boundaries are architecture decisions you make in *any* stack, including a bare Three.js/TypeScript project — they are not something only a "real engine" provides. Three.js + TypeScript + Vite already gives Mythoras a render loop, a scene graph, and a module system; what's missing is discipline about who owns what, which is free to build now and doesn't require inventing engine infrastructure.
2. **The "grows into a real engine" framing is a trap for a small/solo-adjacent team specifically because engines earn their complexity from generality across many games — Mythoras only needs generality across its own future feature set.** Building toward hypothetical future genericity (which is what "could grow into a real engine" implies) is exactly the kind of over-planning the owner has flagged wariness about elsewhere in this project (per the owner-working-style note) — it's speculative infrastructure investment before the thing it's infrastructure *for* has been validated to be fun.
3. **The correct middle path, and what the Godot video is actually demonstrating, is "build a thin, purpose-built application shell with clear ownership boundaries, and let systems earn their way into shared/reusable code only once you've written the same thing twice."** Concretely for Mythoras: a coordinator module that owns the top-level scene graph structure (world root, entity root, UI layer stack, debug overlay) the same way the video's `main game` node does; a `loadEncounter()`/`loadScene()`-style single entry point that any trigger (menu, travel, story event) goes through, so transition logic never has to be duplicated per call site; entities (creatures) registered in one place so debug tooling can always answer "what's alive right now" without digging through nested state. None of this requires calling it an "engine" or planning for reuse beyond Mythoras itself — it's just not skipping the foundation step the video's author explicitly says he learned to regret skipping.
4. **The risk of *not* doing this minimal structuring is concretely worse for a squad-RPG than for many other genres**, because Mythoras's core loop (Planning Phase queuing actions for a squad, Execution Phase resolving them with interleaved enemy initiative) touches nearly every system at once — UI, entity state, turn sequencing, VFX — which is precisely the shape of problem that turns into "spaghetti code" fastest if ownership isn't decided early. This raises the priority of doing basic structural setup soon, not the priority of building toward a reusable engine.

## 6. Prioritized build list

Filtered through: cheap to prototype roughly + reveals whether a core system feels right, ranked above what would look best. Each item names the reference(s) it draws from. This list assumes the current bare Three.js scaffold (orthographic camera, ground plane, placeholder cylinder markers for 3 Field + 2 Bench) as the starting point.

**1. State the question, then build the Turn Program loop with placeholder boxes only — no art.**
Queue actions for 3 Field creatures in a Planning Phase, resolve them with enemy-initiative interleaving in an Execution Phase, using the existing cylinder markers (or simpler) as the only visual. Before writing code, write down the specific question this prototype answers (Thronefall §5.3's discipline) — most likely: "does watching a queued plan resolve, with enemy actions interleaved, feel like a decision I made, or feel like watching a cutscene?" *Draws from: Thronefall devlogs 0–2 (state the question, prototype disposably), Thronefall devlog 3 ("fix fun first"), MoP battle systems (the bar for what "informed tactical decision" needs to feel like).*

**2. Decide and prototype enemy-intent visibility as its own isolated experiment.**
Before any art, build 2–3 debug-text or debug-shape variants of "how much do I show about what enemies will do" (nothing, banded/symbolic, exact) inside the Turn Program loop from item 1, and get reactions from people who are not the primary designer. This is the single most directly evidenced "don't trust your own taste" lesson in the whole reference set. *Draws from: Slay the Spire's four-iteration intent-system history (§5.1), MoP battle systems as an existence proof that some intent visibility is expected in the genre.*

**3. Stand up minimal ownership/structure in the Three.js/TS codebase before more systems land on top of the scaffold.**
A coordinator module owning world/entity/UI-layer roots, a single scene/encounter-load entry point, and a central entity registry — small, not a framework. This is cheap now and expensive to retrofit once the Turn Program (item 1) and split-focus UI (item 4) both exist and need to talk to shared state. *Draws from: Godot project-structure video (§5.5), directly answers the "build our own engine" question as "no, but do this much."*

**4. Prototype 2–3 split-focus layout skeletons with placeholder boxes, not final art.**
Test a DD-style horizontal band, a vertical split, and a floating/dockable panel arrangement, each showing only fake squad-state data (HP numbers, a status icon) alongside the existing 3D scene. The goal is picking a layout *shape* the team is confident in before any real UI styling work begins. *Draws from: Darkest Dungeon's split-focus principle (§2.1) — explicitly not its ratio; the brief's explicit invitation to consider non-DD layouts.*

**5. Build the roster-band-that-never-disappears as a single shared component, wired into whatever placeholder scenes exist (camp/travel stand-ins are fine as blank rectangles).**
Doesn't need real camp/travel scenes yet — just prove the same UI component instance can be shown across different "screens" (even placeholder ones) without becoming two divergent implementations. *Draws from: Darkest Dungeon's persistent roster principle (§2.2).*

**6. Prototype whether Field-internal position matters mechanically, as a pure logic test on the existing markers.**
Cheapest possible version: does moving a marker from Field-slot-A to Field-slot-B change anything the player cares about (damage taken, skill availability)? Answer this before any position-dependent skill design gets built, since it changes how skills should be authored. *Draws from: Darkest Dungeon's position-in-line-as-mechanical-fact principle (§2.3).*

**7. Prototype the skill-as-equippable-object system as a numbers-only harness (spreadsheet-tier UI is fine).**
Test whether assigning skills (with some rollable variance, per the PoE-gearing analogy) to 5 creatures produces genuinely interesting decisions, independent of any visual representation. This validates or invalidates the PoE-flavored gearing-via-skills idea before any content is authored around it. *Draws from: PoE gearing system (§3.1) and crafting/grafting steps (§3.2) — validating the underlying decision-density claim cheaply before committing content-authoring effort.*

**8. Build a dumb-random automated fuzzer for the Turn Program loop once item 1 exists.**
A bot that queues uniformly random valid actions each Planning Phase and runs thousands of encounters — cheap to build, and it will surface Turn Program edge cases (deadlocks, resolution-order bugs) long before any human playtester would find them. *Draws from: Slay the Spire 2's "Auto Slayer" (§5.2).*

**9. Defer, but architect for: passive-tree-style progression and PoE-style endgame replay knobs.**
Not a near-term build item — explicitly sequenced after the core Turn Program (item 1) is validated. But keep skill/creature data generation parameterized (rarity tiers, roll ranges) from the start (item 7 forces this anyway), so this doesn't require a rewrite later. *Draws from: PoE passive tree (§3.4) and endgame/SSF replay depth (§3.6).*

**10. Defer entirely for now: any real art pass, VFX polish, or tile-anchored persistent-effect rendering.**
Explicitly gated behind items 1–2 proving the core loop is fun. When it does happen, MoP's tile-anchored VFX principle (§1.1) and restrained-palette aesthetic (§1.2) are the right reference points — but there's no reason to invest here before the Turn Program and intent-visibility questions are answered, per the owner's own stated prototype philosophy.

## 7. Fable's own design point of view

This section is deliberately separate from the synthesis above — it's my own read, not a restatement of what the reference games do well. Weigh it against your own instincts; it's meant to be compared against, not deferred to.

**If I were prioritizing Mythoras right now, I'd collapse the first four weeks of "prototype work" into a single artifact: a text-and-box version of one full combat encounter, start to finish, played against a friend or a stranger, not against yourself.** Everything in the dev-philosophy research points the same direction — Slay the Spire's team, with QA backgrounds and genuine design taste, still got the intent-visibility question wrong on their own instincts three separate times before outside testers corrected them; Thronefall's team burned 30 days on a system that "felt like a math problem" specifically because they skipped this step. I'd treat this as close to a hard rule rather than a suggestion: no visual polish work happens until at least one person outside the core team has played a full Turn Program cycle and you've watched their face while they did it.

**I'd be more skeptical than the synthesis above about how early to commit to "3 Field + 2 Bench" as internally position-sensitive (item 6 in §6).** Darkest Dungeon's rank system is elegant, but it's also one of the more subtle sources of new-player confusion in that game — a lot of DD's early frustration for newcomers is "wait, why can't I use this skill, I don't understand the rank math yet." A 5-unit squad is small enough that adding positional sub-rules risks making the tactical surface feel fussier than it is deep. My instinct, to be validated by item 1's prototype rather than assumed: start with Field-vs-Bench as the *only* positional axis, ship that, and only add intra-Field position if the fuzzer/playtesting from items 1–2 shows Field-vs-Bench alone doesn't produce enough tactical texture. Adding depth later is cheap; removing a mechanic players have already built mental models around is not.

**I'd push back gently on treating the PoE-flavored skill-as-gear system (§3.1, §6 item 7) as equally load-bearing as the Turn Program itself.** It's a genuinely good direction — the brief's framing of skills as equippable objects already implies it — but PoE's affix system works because PoE has an enormous, decades-deep item pool and a playerbase numbering in the millions turning over that pool constantly. A prototype-stage Mythoras with a small hand-authored skill pool risks the *appearance* of PoE-style depth (rollable sub-stats, rarity tiers) without the actual combinatorial mass that makes PoE's system feel alive rather than arbitrary. I'd validate the decision-density claim (item 7) with a genuinely small pool first — a dozen skills, minimal variance — and only invest in generating larger variance once it's clear the underlying "assigning skills to creatures" decision is fun even at small scale. If it's not fun with 12 skills, more variance won't fix it; it'll just take longer to notice.

**The thing I'd be most wary of across this entire research pass is treating "Protocol" (compiled/structural power) as a purely cosmetic reskin applied after mechanics are locked.** Nothing in the fixed facts forces Protocol to actually shape any system — it could easily end up as flavor text over a generic elemental-magic skeleton, the same trap the brief explicitly warns against for the reference games themselves ("nothing alien or obscured, because it was copied from something we didn't fully digest"). If Protocol is meant to be a real identity pillar, I'd want at least one mechanical consequence of "compiled/structural" baked into the Turn Program prototype itself, early — for example, skills that must be "assembled" from components before they're usable, or effects that persist as literal installed state on a creature/tile (which pairs naturally with MoP's tile-anchored VFX principle from §1.1) rather than firing once and disappearing. Otherwise there's a real risk of building the entire core loop first and discovering afterward that Protocol never had anywhere to attach mechanically, at which point it becomes a naming exercise rather than a design pillar.

**Finally, on the engine question (§5.5): I agree with the "no" conclusion, but I'd go one step further — I'd actively resist the temptation to name any of the early structural work an "engine layer" or "framework," even internally.** The moment a coordinator module gets called "the engine," it invites investment disproportionate to what it needs to do right now (a handful of ownership boundaries in a TypeScript project). Call it what it is — app structure — and let it stay small until a second or third game-shaped reason to generalize it actually shows up, which for a single-game small-team project may never happen, and that's fine.
